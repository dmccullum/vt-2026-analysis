Vermont's 2026 election: an analysis of the results

What stood out

    Scott fell much further than other Republicans

    Every Republican running statewide lost ground from 2024, but Scott lost far more. The other Republicans dropped by 5 to 12 points, and the U.S. House race barely moved. In 2024 Scott ran ahead of the other Republicans on the ballot. This year he ran ahead, so about one voter in five still chose Scott and Democrats for the other offices.

    What the biggest shifts had in common

    The towns that moved hardest toward Janoo had more college graduates, more people working from home, and higher 2024 turnout. Most of that overlaps with how Democratic a town already was. A near-uniform swing from each town's 2024 result explains 93% of the variation (R² 0.926), and adding eight demographic measures raises that only to 0.931. The one measure that clearly matters is second homes: towns with more seasonal housing held up slightly better for Scott.

    Legislative candidates who ran ahead of or behind expectations

    Each candidate in a contested race is compared with what a typical candidate of the same party would be expected to get in that district. The expectation is based on how the district voted in the six statewide and federal races other than governor, whether the candidate was an incumbent, and how many candidates ran. The difference, in percentage points, is like a wins above replacement (WAR) score: positive means the candidate ran ahead of their party's usual showing. Races with an independent candidate, or with more Democrats or Republicans than seats, are left out because the comparison doesn't work for them.

    Method and data notes

    Results. The Vermont Secretary of State's unofficial election-night files (, towns). SOS reports every race by town and House district, so the statewide races are added up exactly over each legislative district. Royalton, Stannard, and Washington had not reported. Races in districts that include those towns are flagged incomplete. Marlboro's governor return shows Scott at 0 votes and 129 "other write-in" votes, while the town's other races look normal. That looks like a reporting error, so Marlboro is left out of every governor comparison. 2024 results come from the SOS official 2024 files. Governor results from 2016 to 2022 come from the vt-needle baseline (OpenElections and the Vermont Election Archive).

    Partisan lean. For each town or district, the Republican share of the vote for Republican vs. Democratic or Progressive candidates in the races for Lt. Governor, Treasurer, Secretary of State, Auditor, Attorney General, and U.S. House, added together. This is the "other Republicans" and "other Democrats" benchmark used throughout. "Scott vs. other Republicans" is Scott's share of the Scott–Janoo vote minus that number. Shares labeled "two-candidate" leave out minor candidates.

    Above expected (WAR). For each contested Democratic, Progressive, or Republican legislative candidate, share = votes ÷ voters in the district. SOS's ballot count counts each seat, so it is divided by the number of seats. A regression weighted by district size predicts share from the party's partisan lean in the district, party, incumbency (won the same chamber in 2024, matched by name), and independents in the race. House one-seat and multi-seat races are modeled separately, because in multi-seat races voters choose several candidates; the multi-seat model also accounts for how many candidates each party ran. The Senate, with fewer races, uses one model with a multi-seat indicator. The score is actual minus predicted. House: n=, R²=. Senate: n=, R²=. Treat differences under about 3 points as noise.

    Demographics. 2020–2024 American Community Survey 5-year estimates for county subdivisions (Census table-based summary files) and land area from the 2024 Census Gazetteer. These are town-level averages. A pattern across towns does not show how individual voters behaved. There was no 2026 exit poll for Vermont. In the Vermont Public / Vermont State University poll (fielded Sept. 10–21, 732 likely voters), Janoo led 45–38 among likely voters with a bachelor's degree or more and Scott led 51–16 among those with high school or less, which fits the town pattern here.

    Income. Income is ACS per-capita income: total income in the town divided by all residents. It was chosen over median household income, which runs high in commuter towns with two-earner families (Milton $119k, Fairfax $129k ± $29k) and low where many people live alone or with roommates. It was also chosen over median earnings per worker, which counts part-time student jobs and puts Burlington at $26k. Per-capita income counts children, so towns with many families look somewhat poorer. All three measures give the same result: at the same education level, Janoo did better, gained more, and ran closer to the other Democrats in lower-income towns.

    Quintiles. Towns are ranked and split into five groups with equal shares of the 2026 governor vote. Density tiers: rural <50 people per sq mi, small town 50–200, suburban or large town 200–1,000, urban 1,000+.
